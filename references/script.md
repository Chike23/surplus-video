# The story and the script

Read all of this before step 1 (the ideas) and again before step 4 (the script). You write the script from the idea
the user picked. It meets the script-side specs in `references/specs.md`, and every real fact in it is checked and sourced. A script
the user pastes instead is used word for word: only lay it out (the file format below) and check it.

The job: a stranger mid-scroll stops in the first second, can't leave before the end, and walks away with something
they'll repeat to a friend. The story is the video.

## Story first, Surplus last (owner's rule, 2026-11-15)
Work forwards, never backwards from the app. Pick the idea and write the whole story from hook to callback (step 4)
for one thing only: how captivating it is. Never ask "how do we get to Surplus?" while choosing or writing it.
Only once the whole story is written do you look for a bridge to Surplus. A bridge never changes a story beat. If no bridge
is good enough, the video ends on a plain plug instead ("Surplus, last", below).

**The format comes from eight model videos** the owner picked (millions of views each), measured word by word on
2026-11-14: the hook, the way it's told, the engine and the beat map below. Use their *patterns*; never copy their
lines or their stories.

## The two kinds (the user picks one first)
**Twist: a story with a twist.** A gripping story from any era, with a physical cause and effect you can show on a
stage, and a last beat that flips what we thought was going on. It can be true, a true story made better, or made up
(next section). It is a story first: it would be worth watching with no app at the end. Most are about eating, food,
appetite, strength, growing or the body. A few can be any great story with some thread to eating or gaining.

**Relatable: an informative story the viewer lives.** A moment the viewer has had ("you order the big meal and tap out
at half"), the surprising fact or mechanism behind it (always real), and one character living it until the turn
(usually made up). The viewer
recognises themselves in the first three seconds and leaves knowing something they didn't. It's still a story first;
Surplus comes after, as the bridge or the plug. The models' informative videos pull the viewer in with "you" ("you know that feeling when…",
"your stomach…"), so Relatable uses "you" in the body; the hook still starts with the character.

Both kinds turn on **one surprising story, fact or experience.** Never a tip list, never a generic "how to gain weight"
explainer, never a story that's only there to get to the app.

## Where the story comes from: made up or reworked first
Stories don't have to be true, and made-up or reworked ones usually beat true ones, because we can sharpen them. Pick
whatever makes the most captivating video:
- **Fiction:** made up from scratch: a fable, a what-if, a character in an invented town or kingdom. Told straight, as
  if it happened, or as "legend has it". Anything goes, as long as the cause and effect feel real and can be shown on
  stage.
- **Inspired by a true story or a real topic:** take a real event, topic or fact and put our twist on it: a sharper,
  better-planted twist, higher stakes, a new everyman character or setting, bent wherever it makes the story more
  captivating. Swap the real people for invented characters, so nothing made up is pinned on a real person.
- **True:** it happened as told, and only when the true story beats a made-up one. Check its key facts with a web
  search. Real historical people are fine (never living people), but called by what they are ("the king", "the
  scientist"), not by name.

What stays true in every kind: the food, body and weight facts the story teaches (the stomach adapts, a handful of
nuts is about 160 calories), because viewers act on them, so they're checked and sourced; and what Surplus does
(`references/surplus.md` only). Never call a changed or made-up story true ("true story", "this really happened"),
and never pin a made-up event on a real date or a real person. Telling it straight ("The cook ate double rations and
stayed the thinnest man aboard") is fine: that's storytelling, not a claim. A legend is told as a story or as "legend
says", never as fact.

## What makes an idea right for this format
- **Captivating on its own.** Would a stranger stop, stay to the end and retell it? That's what decides an idea.
  Whether or how it reaches Surplus plays no part in picking it.
- One surprising story with a physical cause and effect you can show: something happens to an object or a body, on
  stage, that the viewer can see.
- **A main character who does something that looks wrong** (pointless, crazy or backwards) for a reason we only learn
  at the end. That act is the hook.
- A clear everyman character (a baker, a soldier, a student, a ship's cook, a lighthouse keeper), stakes that escalate,
  and a twist in the last story beat.
- **One of the models' two arcs** (under the engine below): the underdog everyone laughs at who turns out right, or
  the plan that works and then backfires.
- **One central object** the whole story turns on (five bowls, a bell, a mirror, a ladle), there in the hook and in
  the last story line.
- A setting built from simple 3D shapes plus cut-out cards: towns, bridges, ships, castles, deserts, labs, palaces,
  kitchens, gyms, school halls.
- **Twist:** mix the 10 across eras and themes: ancient, medieval, 1800s, modern, science, animals, inventions,
  disasters, weird laws, lucky accidents.
- **Relatable:** mix the 10 across everyday moments: school or work lunch, family dinner, eating out, the gym, sport,
  mornings, a date, holidays, travel, being sick, a growth spurt.
- **Mix the sources and label each:** Twist ideas mostly fiction or a real topic with our twist, and at most 2 of the
  10 true, unless the user asks for one kind; Relatable ideas usually have a made-up character living a real fact.
  A true idea's story is checked with a web search before it's offered; every idea's food or body fact is checked.
- **Rules:** stories, characters and shots are always original. Never recreate another creator's video shot for shot;
  if the user brings one, offer an original take on the same idea. No real, named living people as characters, and
  no real living person as the butt of a joke. No brand mascots or logos. Nothing graphic, nothing political.
- Not too similar to a done story (`references/stories-done.md`), and not too similar to another of the 10.

## The hook: the first four seconds decide everything
All eight models open the same way: **one strange thing the main character did, said as plain fact in the past
tense**, then, within the next two to five seconds, **a second beat that piles on the pressure**. It opens one
question (why would anyone do that?), and only the end answers it.
- **The act:** the first word is He, She or They (the main character's pronoun, whichever fits best), then a strong
  verb and one specific object, in the simple past, 12 words or fewer: "He split his dinner into five tiny bowls."
  It looks wrong, pointless or crazy, and it's the strangest thing in the story. The first frame shows it happening.
- **The pressure beat, in the same breath:** the crowd that mocks it ("and the whole barracks laughed"), what's at
  stake ("and he bet his whole pay on it"), or a detail that makes it stranger ("and not just any bowls"). Joined with
  "and" or "but".
- **Then drop into the present tense** ("so he asks the cook…"). The hook is the only past-tense line.
- **Start in the middle**, at the moment it's already strange. No set-up, no name ("Meet Sam", "This is Sam"),
  no "Did you know", "Have you ever", "Here's why", "In 1854,", no question, no title card.
- **A picture, not a concept.** "He split his dinner into five tiny bowls." not "Soldiers had strange eating habits."
- **A number only if it shocks and can be pictured.**
- **Relatable hooks give the character the viewer's own habit, made strange:** "He ate until he was stuffed, every
  single meal, and he still lost weight."
- **Write ten hooks for the picked idea, keep the best,** and test it: would a stranger mid-scroll stop? Can the first
  frame show it? Does it promise something only the end delivers? Is any real fact in it checked? Any "no" means
  rewrite.

Hook shapes (adapt; never copy anyone's line; the facts in these are placeholders, and a real fact you use is checked):
- **The act that looks crazy, and the crowd that laughs:** "He <did a strange thing to one object>, and <the crowd>
  laughed."
- **The act, and it gets stranger:** "They <did a strange thing>, and not just any <object>."
- **The act, and the stakes:** "He bet <everything he had> on <the worst choice>."
- **The sensible act that backfired:** "She <did the sensible thing>, and it <made it worse>."
- **The viewer's habit, flipped:** "He skipped breakfast because he wasn't hungry, and his body planned that."

## How it's told: like telling your best friend
Not a description of an event, not a narrator reading a story: you, telling your best friend what happened, the
moment you heard it.
- **Present tense, everyday words:** "so he walks up to the guard", "and he just eats it". Say it the way you'd say it
  out loud. The models open sentences with **So, And, But, Then, Until, Instead, Meanwhile, Wait, Well**, and lean on
  "just" and "right". **"Then" brings a turn** ("Then weigh-in day comes"); "and then" never strings a list.
- **Voice the viewer.** Once or twice a video, the teller says what the viewer is thinking, echoing the strangest
  word, and answers it at once with "Well,": "Wait, five bowls? Well, …", "Wait, what? Well, nobody can explain it."
  It's an aside: said lower and closer, never shouted.
- **Comment like a friend, about every ten seconds:** one clause of hyperbole ("the guy the wind used to knock
  over"), understatement ("which is not ideal") or a dry aside ("at least in his head"). It keeps it funny between
  the beats. Never at a real person.
- **Voice the doubters.** Two to four times a video, at the turns, the teller says a character's actual line in their
  voice, in quotes, about six words or fewer. In the models these are mostly the people who doubt or mock the main
  character (the cook, the boss, the crowd), and a back-and-forth between two of them makes a good gag. Attribute it
  casually with "goes" ("and the cook goes, "It's the same stew!"") or not at all when it's clear; never "he said".
  The main character stays calm and mostly quiet: one short calm line at most. The puppet turns and swaps its pose on
  the line.
- **Delivery cues:** a voiced line can start with a short cue in square brackets, `[gruff] "Again."`, `[whispers]`,
  `[shocked]`. voice.mjs sends it to ElevenLabs and leaves it out of the word count and timing. Use one only where the
  voice needs it, and listen to the take: the line must sound acted (pitch and melody, not volume), not read.

## Say it as it is
Name the real thing and the real result, in the plainest words. No metaphors, euphemisms or poetic stand-ins.
- The result is what happened to the person: "he finally gained weight", "he put on three kilos", never "the needle
  finally moved" or "the scale finally listened". (The picture can still show the needle; the words say the result.)
- Things are what they are: a loaf that weighs too little is "one underweight loaf", not "one light loaf"; he "can't
  finish the plate", not "the plate wins".
- **The main character is he, she or they** (whichever fits best), never a name. **Everyone else is what they are:**
  the guards, the painter, the baker, the king, the doctor. No names at all.

## The story engine: keep them watching every second
- **Want, obstacle, stakes by second eight.** We know who, what they want, and what they lose if they fail.
- **One of two arcs.** *Underdog* (five of the eight models): everyone doubts or laughs at the main character, they
  stay calm and keep going, it works, and the doubters are proven wrong. *Backfire* (two of eight): the plan works,
  then it turns ("but it only works for a month"). A Relatable story runs the same way: the habit everyone (the
  viewer too) thinks is right turns out to be the problem.
- **Withhold the why.** We see what they do and what it causes; the reason it works stays hidden until the "You see,"
  reveal near the end. Explaining it early kills the story.
- **"But" and "so", never "and then".** Every beat is caused by the last one and turns it (a setback or a discovery).
- **Climb in threes.** A time ladder, once or twice a video, in one breath, the third step the biggest: "week one…,
  week two…, and by week four…". All eight models use one.
- **Escalate.** Each beat raises the stakes or the strangeness. The biggest beat comes right before the twist.
- **Open loops.** The hook opens the big question. Every 8–10 seconds a signpost opens a smaller one ("now here's the
  weird part", "then summer comes, and something weird happens", "until one night") and the next beat closes it.
- **Plant, then pay off.** A specific detail (a number, a sound, a side, a colour) is planted early and pays off at the
  twist. A twist from nowhere feels cheap; a planted one feels clever and gets rewatched.
- **Hard specifics in every beat:** a role, a place, a number, an object (five bowls, two kilos, week four). One clear
  image per beat.
- **The twist flips the meaning** of what came before, not just a fun fact tacked on. Test: does the hook read
  differently after the twist?
- **End on a callback.** The story's last line brings back the hook's object or crowd, turned around ("And the guys
  who laughed? They all eat from five bowls now."). The models stop right there, and so does the story; then come
  the bridge or the plug.
- **Feeling we can see:** hope, embarrassment, triumph, shown by the puppet (pose swaps, a glance, a slump), never
  named by the narrator.
- **Cut the slack.** A line nobody would miss goes.

## Show, don't tell
The picture tells the story. The narration says only what the picture can't.
- Never name a feeling ("he was embarrassed"). The puppet shows it (a slumped pose swap, a look at the full plate);
  the narration gives the fact that caused it.
- Never describe what's on screen ("here you can see the scale go up"). The needle swings on the word.
- The narration carries what pictures can't: time ("three weeks later"), cause ("because his stomach..."), the
  number, the twist in words.
- **Every line has a picture.** In script.md each line gets a *We see* line under it. If a line has no picture,
  rewrite it until it has one, or cut it.
- **Concepts are shown whole, never left to the imagination.** A line that teaches a fact, a cause or a number gets a
  picture that demonstrates it with things on stage: a number becomes a count of objects, a comparison puts both
  things whole in one frame and visibly different, a process happens step by step, and a body change is seen happening.
  Test: with the sound off, would a viewer understand it? If no picture can do that, rewrite the line.
- **Plan the shot with the line.** Under the *We see* line, a *Camera* line gives the size, what the frame takes in
  and the one thing it's focused on. The first line in each new place also gets a *Scene* line: what's in the place
  (set pieces, characters, props and the state each prop starts in), with the 2–3 things that say where we are
  without a word. A line whose shot won't work is fixed now, before
  the voice is paid for.
- **The picture lands first:** the reaction or reveal happens 0–3 frames before its word, so the eye leads and the
  ear confirms.
- Verbs and objects over adjectives: "He ate the whole loaf" beats "He ate a lot".
- Labels and lettering name things (a sack, a number on a dial). They never repeat the narration as a caption, and
  never name the place or announce what's next or when: the set says where we are, and the picture shows time passing.

## The numbers (references/specs.md)
- Mark on v4 runs at about 3 words a second at his own pace, pauses included, so **45–60 s is about 130–195 words**.
  Aim for 150–170 (about 50–57 s).
- **The hook:** a surprising action, stated as fact, inside the first 4 s (the first ~12 words), on screen from the
  first frame. Aim for 3 s.
- **"You see,"** starts the line right after the hook (the who and the stakes, never the why), or brings the reveal
  60–75 % in (the why). The models use one or both.
- **A new place every ~20 words** (6–7 s): each beat moves somewhere new on the stage.
- **The payoff (the bridge or the plug) lands in the last 5–15 %** (from about word 85–95 % on) and the script ends
  on it. Its last line is the CTA. No outro, no sign-off after it.

## Surplus, last: a bridge or a plug
Written only after the whole story is written (step 4), and it never changes a story beat. Either way, never write a line
that needs the app on screen ("tap here", "look at this screen", "open the app and..."): the picture never shows a
phone or the app. Features only from `references/surplus.md`, and any rate of gain inside its guard rails (0.25–0.5
kg a week).

**The bridge, when the story hands you one.** After the callback the story's trick or object walks into today: the
same puppet in a quick costume swap (or a modern twin with the same problem) is still doing the story's thing, and
Surplus is the one tool that helps them do it. The narration says what Surplus does **for the character**, and the
picture shows them doing it: eating the extra meal, stepping on the scale and finally weighing more, a proud pose.
"Today he's still on five bowls, and Surplus keeps him five hundred calories over, and he's three kilos up. Search
Surplus on the App Store." Same teller, same breath, no "and that's why you need…". About 15–25 words. The character
is the hero and Surplus is the tool they pick up: "he finally ate enough", not "Surplus changed his life".

**The bridge test.** Use a bridge only if all three are true: it grows out of the story's own trick, object or
problem in one breath, with no new set-up and no stretch ("speaking of eating…"); it makes sense to someone who
struggles to gain weight; and it's at least as good an ending as the plug. Write two or three bridges and test them.
If none passes, use the plug: a weak bridge is worse than an honest plug.

**The plug, when no bridge passes** (owner's rule, 2026-11-15). The story ends on its callback, then a straight cut to
a new scene of its own, and one plain line in the same voice, then the CTA. Keep it that simple: say what Surplus is
for, never how it works (no tracking, counting, targets or when to eat). A plug always ends on the comment CTA,
whatever CTA was picked for a bridge: "Comment BULK and I'll send you the app" (the user's own keyword if they picked
the keyword CTA). About 20–25 words in all, along the lines of "If you want to gain weight or bulk, Surplus is a
bulking app that helps you do it. Comment BULK and I'll send you the app." The new scene shows it, never a phone
or the app: a skinny cut-out filling out his shirt, or a scale's needle swinging up, with Surplus and the CTA lettered
large on the marquee.

**Never an ad, either way.** Surplus isn't named, hinted at or set up before the payoff, and with the payoff cut it's
still a story worth sharing. One thing Surplus does, no feature list, no hype words, no price, no "download now".

## Writing for the ear
- One idea per clause, everyday words. The main character as he, she or they, everyone else by what they are, the
  viewer as "you".
- **The models' rhythm:** long flowing sentences (11–25 words, clauses joined with "and", "so", "but", "because")
  broken by a few short punch sentences at the turns ("So he waits.", "Wait, what?"). They talk at 3.3–3.8 words a
  second with only 0.15–0.35 s between sentences: no long pauses anywhere.
- Concrete beats vague: "a handful of almonds is 160 calories" beats "nuts are calorie-dense".
- Numbers as they should be said: "five hundred calories", "half a kilo".
- **No pauses: write it to flow.** The voice is never cut or re-timed. Mark on v4 (measured on the baker video) stops
  about 0.4–0.6 s at a full stop or question mark and 0.15–0.45 s at a comma. So join most beats with commas and
  bridges ("and", "but", "so", "because", "which is", "and get this"), and keep full stops for the hook, a few punch
  sentences at the turns, the callback and the CTA (about a dozen stops of any kind in all). Line breaks in script.md
  don't make pauses; punctuation does.
- **Expressive, not flat:** one long run-on reads monotone. The voiced lines, the viewer's "Wait, …?" and the punch
  sentences give it questions, exclamations and stops, plus a dash before the reveal, so the voice rises and falls.
  The take the owner picked, as the model:
  "...hungry again by three, so he tries one trick, and get this — it's just a handful of nuts between every meal,
  which is six hundred extra calories and he never even feels stuffed! Surplus counted every bite and set his target
  five hundred over, and six weeks later? He'd finally gained weight. Search Surplus on the App Store."
  (The take's ending is reworded here to say it as it is.)
- **The script sets the pace.** v4 ignores the speed setting, so too slow means more bridges and fewer full stops,
  and too fast means a punch sentence at a turn. Short, plain words help too.
- Tone from `references/surplus.md`: a coach who has been skinny too; no hype words, no body-shaming.
